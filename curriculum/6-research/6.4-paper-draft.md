# 6.4 — Writing the Research Paper

**Phase**: 6 — Research & Publication
**Est. reading time**: 15 min

---

## Paper Structure

Every technical database paper follows this skeleton:

```
Abstract           (write LAST — 250 words max)
1. Introduction    (problem + your contribution)
2. Background      (what reader needs to know)
3. Experimental Setup
4. Results         (facts only — no interpretation yet)
5. Discussion      (why, what it means, limitations)
6. Threats to Validity
7. Conclusion
References
```

---

## Writing Each Section

### Abstract

Write last. Answer these four questions in 2–3 sentences each:
1. What problem? (Default PostgreSQL config underperforms on modern NVMe hardware)
2. What did you do? (Systematic benchmarking of N parameters across M workload types)
3. What did you find? (Include actual numbers — "TPS improved 34%")
4. What should readers do differently? (Set random_page_cost=1.1 on NVMe)

**Example:**
> PostgreSQL's default configuration parameters were designed for spinning disk and moderate memory constraints. As NVMe storage and large-memory servers become commodity, default settings increasingly misrepresent the hardware's capabilities, leading to suboptimal query plans and write performance. This paper presents a systematic empirical study of six PostgreSQL configuration parameters across three workload types (OLTP, read-heavy, analytics) on commodity NVMe hardware. We find that setting `random_page_cost = 1.1` improves selective query TPS by 34% on NVMe by enabling index usage the planner otherwise avoids. Increasing `max_wal_size` from 1GB to 8GB reduces checkpoint-related latency variance by 67% on write-heavy workloads. Together, these six parameter adjustments improve aggregate TPS by 45% over PostgreSQL defaults without any hardware changes.

---

### Introduction

Three parts:
1. **Motivation**: Why does this matter? (Performance = cost = user experience. DBAs tune by intuition, not measurement.)
2. **Gap**: What does no one know yet? (No systematic study of parameter interaction on NVMe.)
3. **Contribution**: What specifically did you produce? (Measurements, guidelines, or a new technique.)

End with: "The rest of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides background on..."

---

### Background

Cover what the reader needs to understand your results. For a PostgreSQL paper:
- shared_buffers and the double-buffering issue
- WAL and checkpoints (why max_wal_size matters)
- The query planner cost model (why random_page_cost is wrong on SSD)
- How VACUUM interacts with write performance

Keep it brief. Assume reader knows relational databases but not PostgreSQL internals.

---

### Experimental Setup

Be **extremely precise**. Someone must be able to reproduce your results exactly.

**Hardware section:**
```
CPU: AMD EPYC 7302P (16 cores, 3.0 GHz base)
RAM: 128 GB DDR4-3200 ECC
Storage: Samsung PM9A1 NVMe 1TB (sequential read: 7,000 MB/s, random 4K read: 1M IOPS)
Network: 10 Gbps (not used — single-server benchmark)
```

**Software section:**
```
OS: Ubuntu 22.04.3 LTS, kernel 5.15.0-89-generic
PostgreSQL: 16.1
pgbench: bundled with PostgreSQL 16.1
OS tuning applied:
  vm.swappiness = 1
  vm.dirty_bytes = 536870912
  THP disabled
  I/O scheduler: none (NVMe)
```

**Benchmark parameters:**
```
pgbench scale factor: 500 (~7 GB working set)
Clients: 20
Threads: 8
Duration: 300 seconds per run
Warmup: 60 seconds (discarded)
Runs per configuration: 3
```

---

### Results

State facts. No interpretation yet.

Use tables for raw numbers:

| shared_buffers | Mean TPS | Std Dev | Cache Hit Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 GB | 487.3 | 12.4 | 71.2% |
| 2 GB | 612.1 | 9.8 | 82.4% |
| 4 GB | 721.4 | 8.1 | 94.7% |
| 8 GB | 783.2 | 7.3 | 99.1% |
| 12 GB | 784.9 | 8.9 | 99.4% |
| 16 GB | 785.1 | 9.2 | 99.5% |

Use figures for trends (line graphs, bar charts). Reference them: "As shown in Figure 1..."

**Write exactly what happened**: "TPS increased from 487 (1 GB) to 783 (8 GB), a 60.8% improvement. Beyond 8 GB, TPS did not increase further (784 at 12 GB, 785 at 16 GB), suggesting the 7 GB working set fits in the buffer pool at 8 GB."

---

### Discussion

Now interpret. Ask:
- Why did this happen? (Technical explanation from your internals knowledge)
- What is the practical implication for a DBA? ("Set shared_buffers to 25% of RAM if working set fits")
- When does this NOT apply? (If working set is larger than any feasible buffer, or if RAM is heavily shared with other processes)

**Be honest about surprises**: "Contrary to our hypothesis, increasing work_mem beyond 256MB did not improve latency further. We believe this is because..."

---

### Threats to Validity

This section is often skipped by beginners and noticed immediately by reviewers. Include it.

- **Hardware specificity**: Results on Samsung PM9A1 may not generalize to all NVMe drives.
- **Workload specificity**: pgbench TPC-B may not represent your production workload.
- **Short duration**: 5-minute runs may not capture long-running behaviors (vacuum, checkpoint cycles).
- **Single-node**: Results may differ in replication setups due to WAL amplification.
- **PostgreSQL version**: Findings are specific to PostgreSQL 16.1; internal behavior may differ in other versions.

---

### Conclusion

3–4 sentences:
1. Restate the problem
2. Summarize your key findings (with numbers)
3. Practical takeaway
4. Future work direction

---

## Tools

**Writing**: LaTeX (if targeting academic venues), Markdown (for blog/arXiv preprint), Google Docs (for collaboration).

**Figures**: matplotlib (Python), R + ggplot2, or gnuplot.

**References**: BibTeX with JabRef or Zotero.

**arXiv submission**: PDF from LaTeX or any PDF tool. Free. Immediate. Gets a DOI.

---

## The Minimum Viable Paper

If the full paper feels overwhelming, start with:
1. One experiment (e.g., shared_buffers)
2. 3 runs of 5 minutes each
3. A clear hypothesis and result
4. 4–6 pages

Post to arXiv. Get it out. Then expand.
