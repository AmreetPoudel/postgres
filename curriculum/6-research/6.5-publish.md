# 6.5 — Peer Review & Publication

**Phase**: 6 — Research & Publication
**Est. reading time**: 10 min

## The Publication Path

### Step 1: Internal Review

Before submitting anywhere, get someone who knows PostgreSQL to read it.

Questions to ask your reviewer:
- "Can you identify any methodological flaw?"
- "Do my conclusions follow from my data?"
- "What would you change about the experimental setup?"
- "Would you change any config based on this?"

Revise before submitting externally.

---

### Step 2: arXiv Preprint

Post to arXiv first. It's free, you get a DOI, and papers usually appear within a day or two after moderation. First-time submitters to a category need an endorsement from an established arXiv author (arXiv tightened these rules in 2026), so line one up before you need it.

```
https://arxiv.org/
Category: cs.DB (Databases)
```

Submit as PDF. Your paper gets an arXiv ID like `arXiv:2610.12345 [cs.DB]`. This is citable immediately. Many conference reviewers appreciate a preprint link in the submission — shows your work is serious.

---

### Step 3: Conference Submission

**Academic venues:**
- **VLDB** (Very Large Data Bases) — most prestigious database conference
- **SIGMOD** — ACM flagship database conference
- **USENIX ATC** — good for systems-oriented work
- **DBTest (SIGMOD workshop)** — specifically for database testing/benchmarking research

**PostgreSQL community venues:**
- **PGConf** (US, EU, Asia) — practitioner-focused, good for applied research
- **pgDay** events (regional)
- **PGConf.dev** — developer-focused, highly technical audience

**Practical first target**: PGConf talk (15–45 minute presentation) + arXiv paper. This gets your work in front of the community that will use it, and gives you experience presenting before submitting to a competitive academic venue.

---

### Step 4: Community Distribution

After publishing:

```markdown
1. Write a blog post:
   - Shorter, less formal than the paper
   - Focus on "what does a DBA do with this?"
   - Link to the full paper

2. Post to:
   - Hacker News: "PostgreSQL NVMe tuning — empirical study"
   - Reddit r/PostgreSQL
   - PostgreSQL mailing lists (pgsql-performance)
   - LinkedIn (database community)

3. Submit to:
   - PostgreSQL Weekly newsletter (postgresweekly.com)
   - DB Weekly

4. Talk at local meetup or online
```

## Responding to Reviewers

When you submit to a conference, you'll receive reviews. They will find problems with your work. This is normal and valuable.

**Rules for responding:**
1. Address every comment, even if you disagree
2. For each comment: state what reviewer said, your position (agree/disagree), what you changed
3. Never be defensive or dismissive
4. Thank reviewers (silently, in the revision letter)

**Format for each response:**
```
Reviewer 2, Comment 3:
"The authors should explain why they chose 20 clients rather than testing
a range of concurrency levels."

Response: We agree this is an important variable. We have added Experiment 3
(Section 5.3) testing 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 concurrent clients. Results show
that at 20 clients, the system was at ~70% of CPU saturation, providing
meaningful load without being compute-bound. We chose 20 as a representative
value for the main experiments.
```

## Making Work Reproducible

Reproducibility is increasingly required by top venues and respected by the community.

Post everything to GitHub:

```
postgres-research/
├── research/
│   ├── benchmarks/
│   │   ├── run_shared_buffers_exp.sh
│   │   ├── run_checkpoint_exp.sh
│   │   └── analyze_results.py
│   ├── data/
│   │   ├── exp1_shared_buffers_raw.csv
│   │   ├── exp2_random_page_cost_raw.csv
│   │   └── hardware_specs.md
│   └── paper/
│       ├── paper.pdf
│       └── paper.tex
└── README.md        ← How to reproduce the experiments
```

In your README:
```markdown
## Reproducing Results

Requirements: PostgreSQL 16.1, pgbench, Python 3.9+, 64+ GB RAM, NVMe storage

1. Clone this repo
2. Initialize pgbench: `pgbench -i -s 500 -d pgbench_db`
3. Run experiment 1: `bash research/benchmarks/run_shared_buffers_exp.sh`
4. Generate figures: `python3 research/benchmarks/analyze_results.py`
```

## What Makes a Good First Paper

**Good scope**: One clear question, answered well. Not "we studied all PostgreSQL parameters."

**Good contribution**: Either (1) empirical results that didn't exist before, or (2) a practical guideline that is better justified than "we've always done it this way."

**Good writing**: Abstract states numbers. Introduction is one page. Results are tables and figures. Discussion explains the why.

**Good reproducibility**: Someone with the same hardware can run your scripts and get your numbers.

You don't need a novel algorithm or theoretical breakthrough. Measurement papers — "here is what actually happens when you do X" — are highly valued because they're honest and useful.
